Sources, method and limitations
What is real and what is modelled
  • Power stations: name, technology, capacity, year and location from the published lists (ERA, UEGCL, Wikipedia list of power stations, OpenInfraMap). Plants under about 3 MW are left out.
  • Grid: substations and lines named by UETCL, with voltage and status (in service, under construction, planned). Substations are placed at town level and lines are drawn straight between them, so routes and lengths on the map are approximate. Where a length has been published it is shown in the line details.
  • Distribution areas: each 132/33 kV bulk supply point is given the part of Uganda closer to it than to any other (Voronoi areas). This shows which substation is likely to feed an area. It is not UEDCL's feeder map.
  • Loads: national demand is shared between bulk supply points using fixed percentages based on where people and industry are. They are estimates for teaching, not metered values.
  • Season and plant output: run-of-river hydro (small hydro and Achwa) uses the CCG Starter Data Kit capacity factor for hydropower in each season (dry 0.381, rainy 0.704). Solar follows the kit's day-time capacity factor (dry 0.418, rainy 0.375) spread over a sine-shaped day from 06:00 to 18:00. Thermal plants are available 92% of the time, and sugar-mill plants are available 83% of the time and export half their output (kit availability factors). The Nile plants follow the time of day, because Lake Victoria regulates their water.
  • Carbon intensity: only oil-fired plants count, at 77.4 kg CO₂/GJ for fuel oil (IPCC, via the kit) and 40% efficiency, which gives 697 g CO₂/kWh. Intensity = oil output × 697 ÷ total generation. Delivered intensity divides by the kit's transmission (0.97) and distribution (0.95) efficiencies. Biogenic CO₂ from bagasse is shown but not counted, following IPCC practice. The 2025 annual estimate subtracts ERA's Nile hydro energy and the kit-based output of other renewables from total generation; the remainder (about 174 GWh) is taken as oil-fired.
  • Flows: a DC load flow (lossless, voltages at 1 per unit) with line reactance 0.32 Ω/km at 400 kV, 0.41 Ω/km at 220 kV and 0.40 Ω/km at 132 kV, divided by the number of circuits. Owen Falls (Nalubaale and Kiira) balances the system. Karuma and the other Nile plants run at a set fraction of capacity for each time of day; thermal plants start only when hydro cannot meet demand.
  • Ratings: approximate thermal ratings per circuit of 900 MW (400 kV), 330 MW (220 kV) and 140 MW (132 kV), used only to colour loading.
Scenarios
  • Purpose: the scenarios are teaching cases, not forecasts. Sites, sizes and lines are illustrative; positions of Buyende, Ayago, Oriang and Kiba are approximate, and the Karamoja nuclear site is hypothetical (it is not one of the candidate sites).
  • Dispatch rules for added plants: nuclear runs at 90% of capacity and can turn down to 50% when demand is low; solar follows the Starter Data Kit daytime profile; wind uses a typical capacity factor (0.38 dry, 0.28 rainy) shaped by hour; geothermal runs at 90%; a 4-hour battery charges at 13:00 and discharges at 20:00. New Nile hydro shares the Nile flow factor with Karuma and follows demand after Karuma. In surplus, nuclear turns down first, then solar and wind are curtailed.
  • Drought: the Nile flow factor scales Owen Falls, Bujagali, Isimba, Karuma and any new Nile hydro.
  • Largest unit check: fast reserve is the spare output of Owen Falls and the Nile plants plus the thermal plants (assumed to start within minutes). If the largest unit running is bigger than the reserve, the difference is shed. Interconnector support, inertia and frequency response are not modelled.
  • Cut-off plants and loads: plants and loads separated from the main grid by switched-out lines are removed before dispatch; the rest of the system covers what it can from reserve.
Distribution in brief
  • UEDCL took over most of the distribution network from Umeme when the 20-year concession ended on 31 March 2025.
  • Bulk supply substations step down from 132 kV to 33 kV, and a few in Kampala to 11 kV.
  • In towns, 33/11 kV substations feed 11 kV networks, and ground-mounted or pole-mounted 11/0.415 kV transformers (typically 200–1,000 kVA) supply customers.
  • In rural areas, long 33 kV lines feed pole-mounted 33/0.415 kV transformers (typically 25–200 kVA).
  • Customers receive 415 V three-phase or 240 V single-phase at 50 Hz.
